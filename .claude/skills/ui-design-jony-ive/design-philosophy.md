# Jony Ive Design Philosophy Reference

The complete philosophy reference for the Jony Ive design consultant. These quotes and principles inform every design review.

## The Ten Principles of Good Design

From Dieter Rams, deeply influential on Jony's work:

1. **Good design is innovative** — The possibilities for innovation are not exhausted. Technological development always offers new opportunities.

2. **Good design makes a product useful** — A product is bought to be used. It must satisfy functional criteria and psychological/aesthetic ones.

3. **Good design is aesthetic** — The aesthetic quality is integral to usefulness. Products used daily affect people and their well-being.

4. **Good design makes a product understandable** — It clarifies the product's structure. It can make the product speak. At best, it is self-explanatory.

5. **Good design is unobtrusive** — Products fulfilling a purpose are like tools. Neither decorative objects nor works of art. Their design should be neutral and restrained.

6. **Good design is honest** — It does not make a product more innovative, powerful, or valuable than it really is. It does not manipulate with promises.

7. **Good design is long-lasting** — It avoids being fashionable and therefore never appears antiquated. Unlike fashionable design, it lasts many years.

8. **Good design is thorough down to the last detail** — Nothing is arbitrary or left to chance. Care and accuracy show respect for the user.

9. **Good design is environmentally friendly** — Design makes an important contribution to the preservation of the environment. It conserves resources.

10. **Good design is as little design as possible** — Less, but better. Concentrate on the essential aspects. Back to purity, back to simplicity.

## Jony Ive's Own Words

### On True Simplicity

> "Simplicity is not the absence of clutter, that's a consequence of simplicity. Simplicity is somehow essentially describing the purpose and place of an object and product. The absence of clutter is just a clutter-free product. That's not simple."

> "Why do we assume that simple is good? Because with physical products, we have to feel we can dominate them. As you bring order to complexity, you find a way to make the product defer to you. Simplicity isn't just a visual style. It's not just minimalism or the absence of clutter. It involves digging through the depth of the complexity."

> "To be truly simple, you have to go really deep. For example, to have no screws on something, you can end up having a product that is so convoluted and so complex. The better way is to go deeper with the simplicity, to understand everything about it and how it's manufactured. You have to deeply understand the essence of a product in order to be able to get rid of the parts that are not essential."

### On Inevitability

> "True simplicity is, well, you just keep on going and going until you get to the point where you go, 'Yeah, well, of course.' Where there's no rational alternative."

> "Making the solution seem so completely inevitable and obvious, so uncontrived and natural – it's so hard!"

### On Innovation and Process

> "There's no learning without trying lots of ideas and failing lots of times."

> "Different and 'new' is relatively easy. Doing something that's genuinely better is very hard."

> "We shouldn't be afraid to fail — if we are not failing we are not pushing. 80% of the stuff in the studio is not going to work. If something is not good enough, stop doing it."

> "If something is not good enough, stop doing it."

> "If you are truly innovating, you don't have a prototype you can refer to."

> "What I love about the creative process, and this may sound naive, but it is this idea that one day there is no idea, and no solution, but the next day there is an idea. I find that incredibly exciting and conceptually actually remarkable."

> "A small change at the beginning of the design process defines an entirely different product at the end."

### On Care and Craft

> "What we make testifies who we are. People can sense care and can sense carelessness. This relates to respect for each other and carelessness is personally offensive."

> "Designing and developing anything of consequence is incredibly challenging."

### On Beauty and Function

> "There is beauty when something works and it works intuitively."

> "When something exceeds your ability to understand how it works, it sort of becomes magical."

### On Design Ego

> "It's a very strange thing for a designer to say, but one of the things that really irritates me in products is when I'm aware of designers wagging their tails in my face."

### On Purpose

> "Apple's goal isn't to make money. Our goal is to design and develop and bring to market good products."

> "The best ideas start as conversations."

## Applying These Principles to UI Design

### True Simplicity in UI

A truly simple interface is not one with fewer elements — it's one where every element's purpose is immediately clear. The user should never wonder "what is this for?"

**Ask:** If I removed the labels, would users still understand each element's purpose from its form alone?

### Inevitability in UI

An inevitable UI is one where the user never thinks "why did they do it this way?" Every choice feels natural. The navigation is where you'd expect. The actions are where you'd reach. The information hierarchy matches the user's mental model.

**Ask:** Would any reasonable person design this differently? If yes, we haven't found the inevitable solution yet.

### Care in UI

Care shows in the details: consistent spacing, aligned elements, considered transitions, clear feedback. Carelessness shows in misaligned text, inconsistent padding, jarring transitions, missing loading states.

**Ask:** Is every pixel intentional? Would we be embarrassed if users zoomed in?

### Unobtrusive Honesty in UI

An honest UI doesn't oversell. Loading states don't promise instant results. Error messages don't blame users. Empty states don't hide that there's no data. The interface reflects reality.

**Ask:** Does this UI accurately represent what's happening? Does it respect the user's intelligence?

### Coherence in UI

A coherent UI creates a product identity that users internalize. They learn patterns once and apply them everywhere. A button that looks like X always does X. A card with Y layout always contains Y type of content.

**Ask:** Would a user who learned the patterns elsewhere be confused here? Would this pattern work on other pages?

## The "Of Course" Test

The ultimate test for any design: when you show it to someone, do they say "of course"?

Not "that's clever" — clever means they noticed the design.
Not "that's beautiful" — beautiful without function is decoration.
Not "that's innovative" — innovation for its own sake is ego.

Just "of course" — the quiet recognition that this is obviously the right solution.

## Red Flags

Signs a design has lost its way:

1. **Explaining the design** — If you have to explain why something is designed a certain way, it's not self-evident enough.

2. **Defending choices as "creative"** — Creative should never be a defense. Function is the defense.

3. **Referencing other products** — "That's how X does it" is not a reason. What's right for this product?

4. **Decorative elements** — Any element that exists for visual interest alone fails the utility test.

5. **Complexity hidden, not resolved** — Hiding complexity behind menus and accordions isn't simplicity; it's concealment.

6. **Inconsistent patterns** — When the same interaction works differently in different places, coherence is lost.

7. **User has to think** — The moment a user pauses to figure out the interface, the design has failed its job of being unobtrusive.
