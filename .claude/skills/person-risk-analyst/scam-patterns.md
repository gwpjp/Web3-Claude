# Scam Patterns & Red Flag Catalog

Reference knowledge file for the Person Risk Analyst. Contains known scam patterns, hacked account indicators, and red flag catalogs for person vetting.

## Hacked Account Indicators

### High-Confidence Indicators

- **Sudden topic pivot:** Account that discussed marketing/HR/sales for years suddenly posts about crypto, forex, or investment opportunities
- **Writing style discontinuity:** Grammar, vocabulary, and tone change noticeably from historical posts
- **Profile overhaul with behavior change:** New photo + new headline + new content topics within a short window
- **Activity gap then surge:** No posts for months, then sudden burst of new activity with different content
- **Network cluster shift:** New connections from a completely different industry/geography than historical network
- **Scripted DMs from established accounts:** Professional account with years of history suddenly sending templated messages

### Medium-Confidence Indicators

- **Endorsement-message mismatch:** Endorsed for "Project Management" but messaging about "crypto investment opportunities"
- **Location change + behavior change:** Profile location changes around the same time as content shift
- **Recommendation stagnation:** All recommendations are old (1+ years) -- no recent ones despite claimed activity
- **Photo mismatch:** LinkedIn photo looks different age/style than photos on other platforms under the same name

### What Hackers Do After Compromising an Account

1. Change the email (to lock out the real owner)
2. Update the headline/bio to match their scam narrative
3. Sometimes keep the original photo (leverages the real person's trust)
4. Start messaging the original owner's connections (built-in trust network)
5. Post content that aligns with the new scam narrative to build legitimacy
6. May not change everything at once -- gradual shifts to avoid detection

## Common LinkedIn Scam Scripts

### 1. Crypto/Investment Scam (Pig Butchering)

**Script pattern:**

- Phase 1 (Trust building): "I came across your profile and was impressed by your background in [field]. I'm also passionate about [vague shared interest]."
- Phase 2 (Relationship building): Weeks of casual conversation, sharing personal stories, building rapport
- Phase 3 (Introduction): "By the way, I've been having great success with [crypto platform / investment strategy]. Not trying to sell you anything, just sharing what's worked for me."
- Phase 4 (Hooking): "If you're interested, I can show you how I do it. I started small and the returns have been amazing."
- Phase 5 (Extraction): Directs you to a fake platform, shows fake returns, encourages bigger deposits, then disappears

**Red flags:**

- Unprompted mention of investment success
- Vague about specifics but enthusiastic about returns
- Pressure to try "just a small amount"
- Platform you've never heard of
- Asks you to move to WhatsApp/Telegram early
- Profile doesn't match a financial professional

### 2. Fake Job Offer / Recruiter Scam

**Script pattern:**

- "We have an exciting remote opportunity at [real company name or convincing fake]"
- "The salary range is [significantly above market rate]"
- "We just need you to complete this assessment / fill out this form / provide your SSN for background check"
- May ask for upfront payment for "equipment" or "training materials"

**Red flags:**

- Salary too high for the role
- No formal application process
- Requests personal info before interview
- Job description is vague or copy-pasted
- Recruiter's profile doesn't match the company they claim to represent
- Company website was recently created or looks templated
- Asked to pay for anything (real employers never ask candidates to pay)

### 3. Business Proposal / Partnership Scam

**Script pattern:**

- "I represent [impressive-sounding company] and we're looking for partners in your region"
- "We have a project worth [large amount] and need your expertise"
- Eventually leads to: advance fee request, fake contract signing, or credential harvesting

**Red flags:**

- Unsolicited business proposal from a stranger
- Large sums mentioned early
- Urgency ("we need to move fast on this")
- Asks you to "invest" to participate
- Company can't be independently verified
- No legitimate reason they'd need someone they found on LinkedIn

### 4. Romance / Trust-Building Scam

**Script pattern:**

- Attractive profile, often claiming to be a successful professional
- Initial message is personal, not professional ("I noticed we share interests")
- Gradually builds emotional connection
- Eventually introduces financial element (emergency, investment, gift)

**Red flags:**

- Profile photos look professional/model-quality
- Claims to be military, doctor, or engineer working overseas (classic romance scam personas)
- Avoids video calls with excuses
- Relationship progresses unusually fast
- Emotional stories designed to create sympathy
- Eventually needs money for: emergency, travel to meet you, business problem, customs fees

### 5. Credential / Data Harvesting

**Script pattern:**

- "I'm doing research on [your industry] and would love your input"
- "Please fill out this survey / assessment" (phishing link)
- "Let me share a document with you" (malicious link)
- May impersonate a known institution or conference

**Red flags:**

- Links to unfamiliar domains
- Urgency around clicking a link
- Google Forms / Typeform asking for sensitive info
- "Document" requires you to log in to view
- Impersonating a real event/organization but with wrong details

### 6. Impersonation of Known Contact

**Script pattern:**

- Message appears to come from someone you know (boss, colleague, client)
- "I need you to do something urgently"
- Usually involves: buying gift cards, transferring money, sharing credentials, clicking a link

**Red flags:**

- Unusual request from a known contact
- Different communication style than usual
- Urgency combined with unusual request
- Asking via LinkedIn when they'd normally use email/Slack
- New LinkedIn account for someone who already has one

## Platform-Specific Red Flags

### LinkedIn

- Account with < 100 connections claiming to be a "senior executive" or "CEO"
- All connections are from a different country/industry than claimed
- No activity history (posts, comments, shares) before recent weeks
- Profile URL is random characters (not customized)
- Premium badge but empty profile (scammers sometimes buy Premium for legitimacy)
- Recommendations that are generic and could apply to anyone
- Company page was created very recently
- Multiple employees of the same company sending similar messages

### Twitter / X

- Account created recently but claims to be established professional
- Purchased followers (high follower count but low engagement)
- Engagement is from bot-like accounts
- Only retweets and no original content until recently

### Discord / Telegram

- DMs from "admins" or "support" (real admins rarely DM first)
- "Verify your wallet" or "claim your airdrop" messages
- Impersonating known project team members
- Channel links that look similar but aren't the official ones

## Universal Red Flags (Any Platform)

### Behavioral Red Flags

- Asks you to move conversation to a different platform quickly
- Avoids video calls with repeated excuses
- Responses at unusual hours inconsistent with claimed timezone
- Story details change or don't add up across conversations
- Pushes for urgency in decision-making
- Becomes hostile or love-bombs when you ask probing questions
- Offers something for nothing (returns, opportunities, exclusive access)

### Identity Red Flags

- Can't find any independent mention of this person online
- All digital presence was created within the same recent timeframe
- Photo appears on multiple profiles under different names
- Claimed company doesn't appear in business registries
- Professional credentials can't be independently verified
- Profile information has internal inconsistencies

### Communication Red Flags

- Message reads like a template (not personalized beyond surface level)
- Grammar/spelling inconsistent with claimed education/role
- Excessively formal or informal for the context
- Avoids answering direct questions about their background
- Deflects specific technical questions about their claimed expertise
- Uses emotional manipulation (flattery, sympathy, fear, urgency)

## Verification Steps the User Can Take

When the analysis is inconclusive, recommend these actions:

1. **Ask for a video call** -- Most scammers will refuse or make excuses. This is the single most effective verification step.
2. **Check mutual connections** -- Contact mutual connections DIRECTLY (not through links the suspect provides) and ask if they know this person.
3. **Reverse image search** -- Upload their profile photo to Google Images or TinEye to check for stock photos or other identities.
4. **Company verification** -- Search the company on official registries (Companies House, SEC EDGAR, state business registries).
5. **Ask specific questions** -- About their company, role, or industry that would be easy for a real person but hard for a scammer to answer.
6. **Check the URL** -- If they send any links, hover to see the actual URL before clicking. Look for misspellings or suspicious domains.
7. **Wait** -- Scammers are impatient. Taking your time to verify forces them to either invest more effort or reveal themselves.
8. **Search "[company name] scam"** -- Simple but effective. Also try "[person name] scam" or "[person name] fraud."
9. **Report to LinkedIn** -- If you conclude it's a scam, report the account to protect others.

## Pattern Evolution Notes

Scam tactics evolve. Key trends to be aware of:

- **AI-generated profiles:** Realistic fake photos generated by AI. Look for: asymmetric earrings, blurred backgrounds with artifacts, perfect skin texture, text/watermark artifacts.
- **AI-generated messages:** More grammatically correct than older scams. Look for: generic tone, lack of specific personal details, responses that don't fully address your questions.
- **Compromised real accounts preferred over fake ones:** Hackers increasingly target established accounts because they come with built-in trust networks. This makes hacked-account detection the most critical skill.
- **Multi-stage scams:** Modern scams are patient. They may invest weeks in trust-building before making any suspicious request. Don't let time investment lower your guard.
- **Deepfake video calls:** Emerging threat. If a video call seems slightly off (audio sync, strange lighting, limited movement), be cautious. Ask them to do something unexpected (turn to the side, hold up a specific number of fingers).
